**Foundations of CS & AI**

**Shared Assignment Rules --- 2026**

These rules apply throughout the three project stages. Read them when
you begin the project and refer to them while working and before
submitting.

**You are not expected to memorize every requirement. You are expected
to consult the instructions, use the checklists, and check your work.**

**1. Read the Instructions and Attachments**

Each assignment contains:

- **General Instructions:** Its purpose and connection to the course
  project.

- **Specific Requirements:** What to do, what to submit, the deadline,
  and assessment information.

- **Quality Checklist:** Questions to help you evaluate and improve your
  work.

Relevant reference documents, rubrics, and checklists are attached to
the assignment for convenient access. Some documents appear repeatedly
because they apply to several assignments.

**You are not expected to memorize every requirement. You are expected
to consult the instructions and attachments while working and before
submitting.** All team members should read them.

**Most assignments have been completed by previous students.** Examples
are available for many deliverables, including project plans, study
guides, slides, videos, and logs. The mandatory GitHub and Markdown
workflow is a major change this year, so previous submissions may not
demonstrate it.

Review previous work early to understand what an assignment can look
like, identify useful approaches, and notice weaknesses you should
avoid. Compare the examples with the current requirements and quality
checklist.

**Previous work is a reference, not the current assignment specification
or a guarantee of quality.** Requirements change, and earlier
submissions may contain errors, omissions, or features that are no
longer required. Do not assume that reproducing an earlier submission's
structure or content will satisfy this year's assignment. Acknowledge
any material you adapt.

Follow the **current assignment instructions and attachments**. Specific
requirements take precedence over these shared rules where an exception
is explicitly stated. If instructions appear to conflict or remain
unclear, ask the teacher early.

**2. Learn the Content and Develop It in Markdown**

Your team will investigate one project module and produce three
connected learning resources:

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Deliverable**   **Main purpose**
  ----------------- ----------------------------------------------------------
  **Study Guide**   Help beginners learn independently through explanations,
                    examples, activities, exercises, and references.

  **PPT Slides**    Support clear visual explanations of important ideas.

  **Video           Guide learners through focused explanations and
  Tutorials**       demonstrations.
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Markdown is the shared content foundation for all three resources.**

Develop and review your explanations, examples, activities, and
references in Markdown before full production. Follow the content-review
requirements for each stage.

Keep the Markdown content updated throughout the project. When a
concept, example, or answer changes, update every affected resource.

Adapt the content to each medium. Slides need visual explanations;
videos need suitable narration and pacing; the study guide must support
independent reading. All three should use consistent terminology and
teach the same underlying content accurately.

**3. Use GitHub Throughout the Project**

Create the team repository at the beginning and use it continuously.

- Keep the shared Markdown content, references, and relevant source
  files organized.

- Provide a README identifying the team, module, project structure, and
  locations of the learning materials.

- Make meaningful commits as work progresses, with messages describing
  the changes.

- Make individual contributions traceable. Do not leave all uploads
  until the deadline.

- Keep the task list and progress information current.

- Ensure the teacher can access the repository and any linked materials.

Where large media files are stored separately, provide clear, working
links.

**Commit counts alone do not demonstrate contribution.** The substance,
quality, and usefulness of the work---and your ability to explain
it---matter.

Keep personal logs and other private information out of publicly
accessible repositories. Submit them through the designated assignment
channel.

**4. Create Materials That Help Beginners Learn**

Across all deliverables, pay attention to four principles:

- **Accuracy:** Verify important claims, definitions, examples,
  calculations, and answers.

- **Beginner-friendliness:** Explain unfamiliar terms and build
  understanding step by step.

- **Active learning:** Include meaningful examples, questions,
  demonstrations, or activities that let learners apply ideas.

- **Coherence:** Organize the material into a clear learning sequence
  and maintain consistency across resources.

Test the examples and activities yourselves. Check that instructions can
be followed and that expected results are correct.

Use visuals to explain ideas. Make text readable, audio understandable,
and navigation straightforward.

More pages, slides, questions, effects, or recording time do not
automatically mean better work. Every component should serve a learning
purpose.

**5. Share Responsibility and Demonstrate Individual Learning**

Assign clear responsibilities, coordinate regularly, and review one
another's work.

- Every member must make a substantive contribution.

- Every member must understand the team's core content and be able to
  explain their own work.

- Production roles do not remove the responsibility to learn the
  subject.

- Report contributions honestly and support them with evidence.

- Raise workload or participation problems early enough to address them.

The designated submission lead submits one copy of each team assignment
unless instructed otherwise. All members should check the submission
before it is sent.

**Each student is also responsible for learning the required course
content outside their team's module.** Use the course resources, other
teams' materials, and mini lectures to support that learning.

**6. Use AI Responsibly, Record Significant AI Use, and Acknowledge
Sources**

AI can help you understand unfamiliar concepts, explore examples, solve
problems, critique drafts, and create or improve learning materials.

**AI should amplify your learning and work. You remain responsible for
everything you submit.**

**A. Use AI with Understanding and Judgment**

- Verify important AI-generated claims against reliable sources.

- Test generated examples, calculations, code, activities, and answers.

- Check that the content is accurate, relevant, and suitable for
  beginners.

- Be able to explain the material you submit and the decisions you made.

- Never fabricate references, results, feedback, work records, or
  contributions.

Using AI to produce a polished result does not, by itself, demonstrate
your understanding or contribution. You must show how you evaluated the
result and used it meaningfully. You do not need to make unnecessary
changes to correct output merely to demonstrate effort.

**B. Record All Significant AI Use**

**All significant AI use must be recorded in the relevant project and
personal logs as evidence of work and learning.**

Significant use includes assistance with understanding important
concepts, research, planning, problem-solving, content generation, code,
diagrams, slides, videos, test questions, or substantial revision.
Routine spelling corrections and minor formatting assistance do not
require individual records.

**Where to record it:**

- **Project log:** Record significant AI use affecting shared project
  content, deliverables, or decisions. Identify the members involved.

- **Personal log:** Record your own significant AI use, explaining your
  contribution, judgment, and learning---including important learning
  activities that did not produce a final deliverable.

- **When an activity belongs in both logs:** Preserve the full evidence
  once and cross-reference it. Each involved student must still explain
  their own role in their personal log.

Keep these records clearly labelled and connected to the relevant tasks.
Record them as the work happens.

**Each AI-use record should include:**

1.  **Date, person, and tool:** Who used which AI tool, and when?

2.  **Purpose:** What were you trying to understand, create, improve, or
    solve?

3.  **Important interactions:** Preserve the relevant prompts and
    outputs using excerpts, screenshots, or an accessible transcript.
    Include important follow-up exchanges that influenced the result.

4.  **Verification and decisions:** Explain how you checked the output,
    what you accepted, revised, or rejected, and why. Include supporting
    evidence where relevant.

5.  **Outcome and learning:** Identify where the result was used, what
    you contributed, and what you learned.

You do not need to submit every message from every conversation. Select
enough evidence to make the significant work understandable and
verifiable. A tool name, a general statement such as "AI helped with the
slides," or a screenshot of the finished product is insufficient.

**C. Support Your Work with Evidence**

Responsible, documented AI use is allowed. Assessment considers the
quality of the result, your understanding, and your demonstrated
contribution.

**If significant AI use is missing from the logs or inadequately
documented, the affected claims of work, contribution, or learning will
be treated as insufficiently supported. This will reduce the relevant
process-documentation and/or individual-contribution scores according to
the published assessment criteria.**

A polished final product does not replace the required evidence. You may
be asked to explain the content, reproduce a key step, or demonstrate
how you verified an AI-generated result.

AI-use records must be authentic. Fabricated prompts, outputs,
verification steps, or reflections constitute false evidence.

**D. Acknowledge Sources and Borrowed Materials**

Acknowledge external explanations, images, diagrams, code, datasets, and
other materials. Provide references specific enough for readers to
locate the original sources.

Previous students' projects may be used as references. Check their
accuracy, acknowledge any material you adapt, and clearly distinguish
your team's contribution from borrowed work.

**An AI-use record does not replace source attribution.** Check
references suggested by AI, confirm that they exist and support the
relevant claims, and cite the sources you actually consulted.

**7. Keep Useful, Authentic Records**

Maintain the project log, meeting summaries, and personal logs while the
work is happening.

- **Project logs** record progress, completed work, responsibilities,
  problems, and next steps.

- **Meeting summaries** record discussions, decisions, and agreed
  actions.

- **Personal logs** record individual work, learning, difficulties, and
  reflection.

Use timestamps and relevant evidence. Screenshots of outcomes, links to
files, and repository changes are more informative than unsupported
claims.

Reference existing evidence where appropriate. Keep records clear and
concise, and follow the assignment's instructions about whether to
submit the current stage or the whole project history.

**8. Submit Complete, Accessible Work on Time**

Check each assignment for its exact deadline, submission location,
required formats, and file limits. Deadlines use **Beijing time
(UTC+8)** unless stated otherwise.

**Every assignment submission must be self-contained. All required
content and evidence must be included directly in the submitted files.
The teacher should be able to read, understand, and assess your work
without following external links.**

Links to GitHub, online documents, cloud storage, AI conversations, or
other locations may provide additional copies, references, or
supplementary information. **They must not replace the content you are
required to submit. Do not assume that the teacher will open them.**

For example:

- Include the actual project plan and task list, even if you also
  maintain them in GitHub Projects or another online tool.

- Submit the required Markdown files and supporting assets, rather than
  only a repository link.

- Submit the actual slides, documents, and videos in the required
  formats.

- Include relevant evidence directly in your logs, such as readable
  screenshots, excerpts, results, and important AI interactions. A link
  to an AI conversation or commit history alone is insufficient.

- Include the instructions, examples, questions, and answers needed to
  understand and assess your learning materials. External resources may
  support further exploration.

**Required content available only through a link will be treated as
missing from the submission unless the assignment explicitly permits an
exception.**

Before submitting:

- Use clear names identifying the team or student, assignment, and
  version.

- Include every required component and any supporting files needed to
  open or use it.

- Submit the requested formats. Markdown source files do not replace a
  required Word, PDF, PPT, or video submission.

- Avoid compressing a single file unnecessarily. When a package is
  required, organize it into clearly named files and folders.

- Open the files from the submission package and check that they work,
  images display correctly, and evidence is readable.

- Check any supplementary links and access permissions.

- Allow time for uploading and resolving technical problems. If a file
  exceeds the upload limit, contact the teacher before the deadline
  rather than replacing it with an external link without permission.

GitHub remains required for ongoing development, version control, and
contribution tracking. **The assignment submission must contain a
complete snapshot of the work to be assessed at that deadline.**

For revised submissions, provide the complete updated version requested,
rather than only changed fragments. Include the improvement summary
where required.

**9. Use Feedback and Check Your Work**

Use teacher feedback, peer review, audience responses, and your own
testing to improve the project.

For revisions, identify what needed improvement, what changed, and where
the change can be seen. Check related materials for the same problem.

Use the attached quality checklist honestly. A checked box should
correspond to something you have actually inspected or tested.

The checklist supports self-review; the rubric explains assessment.
Completing every submission item is necessary, but the quality of the
work and demonstrated understanding also matter.

If you need help, ask early. Show what you have tried, the relevant
work, and the specific point where you became stuck.

**Important Reminder --- AI Use Requires Evidence**

Record all significant AI use in the relevant project and personal logs,
following Section 6. Show the important interactions, how you checked
the results, and your own contribution and learning.

**A polished product does not replace evidence of your work. Missing or
inadequate AI-use records will reduce the relevant assessment scores
according to the published criteria.**
