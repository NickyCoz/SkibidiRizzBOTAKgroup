### B. Define What the Audience Should Learn

Identify one clear learning outcome, such as:

- Explain why a particular strategy works.
    
- Predict the next step or result.
    
- Compare two approaches and justify a choice.
    
- Recognize a limitation or common misconception.
    
- Apply an idea to a small new example.
    

Choose only the concepts necessary to achieve that outcome. Do not spend most of the lecture on background definitions or a broad history of the subject.

Check all explanations, calculations, examples, code, and answers. Be prepared to explain the reasoning yourselves.

### C. Make the Idea Visible

Include at least one substantial **worked example, diagram, demonstration, comparison, or short audience activity**.

For example, you might:

- Trace how binary search narrows a search space.
    
- Compare two strategies on the same small maze.
    
- Derive a conclusion from a few Prolog facts and rules.
    
- Demonstrate how changing training data affects a classifier.
    
- Step through a small program in a computer simulator.
    

Explain what classmates should notice and why it matters.

Keep demonstrations focused. Prepare inputs, settings, and files beforehand. Avoid spending teaching time installing software, logging in, or navigating unfamiliar menus.

If using an online tool, prepare an offline fallback such as screenshots or a short recording.

### D. Check Understanding Through Application

End the teaching sequence with a brief question or task that requires the audience to **use the idea**, rather than merely repeat a definition.

For example:

- “What happens next, and why?”
    
- “Which strategy would you choose for this case?”
    
- “What is wrong with this rule?”
    
- “How would changing the reward affect the agent’s behavior?”
    

Provide enough information to attempt the task. Allow thinking time, collect a manageable response, and explain the answer.

For a large class, use approaches such as a show of hands, choosing between alternatives, a short prediction, or a brief discussion with a neighbor.

**The understanding check and its explanation must fit within the eight minutes.** Finish with a concise takeaway that answers your central question.

### E. Engage the Audience and Follow the Three Rules

Your classmates will listen to many lectures. Give them a reason to pay attention and help them follow your reasoning.

**1. Cut to the chase.**

Open with a meaningful question, problem, or surprising example. Keep greetings, team introductions, and outlines brief.

**2. Show, not tell.**

Make mechanisms, relationships, and results visible. Demonstrate the idea instead of merely saying it is important or useful.

**3. Save the best for last.**

Build toward your strongest application, revealing result, or insight. The final understanding check and takeaway can provide this payoff.

Use humor, storytelling, drama, or memorable examples where they support learning. Avoid effects or jokes that consume time without helping the audience understand.

Make slides and demonstration details readable from the back of the classroom. Speak clearly and explain naturally; **do not read paragraphs from the screen**.
