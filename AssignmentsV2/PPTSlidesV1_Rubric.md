# PPT Slides Assessment Rubric

**Stage 2 — V1.0**

## 1. Purpose and Scoring

Your slides should help a presenter teach your module clearly and engagingly to a large classroom of beginners.

**The slides need not contain every detail in the Study Guide, but they must tell a complete teaching story.** Essential ideas and their connections should be understandable through the slides and accompanying presenter notes.

This rubric assesses the **submitted slides, presenter notes, and supporting materials**. It does not grade a live performance; the status report and mini lecture have their own assessment requirements.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Assessment dimension**|**Maximum points**|
|1. Essential coverage and teaching story|20|
|2. Accuracy and explanatory quality|20|
|3. Visual design and classroom readability|20|
|4. Audience engagement and participation|15|
|5. Presenter notes and teaching readiness|15|
|6. Coherence across the learning system|10|
|**Total**|**100**|

For each dimension, the teacher selects the best-fitting performance level and awards points within its range.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Level**|**Percentage of the dimension’s available points**|
|**Outstanding**|90–100%|
|**Excellent**|75–89%|
|**Good**|60–74%|
|**Fair**|40–59%|
|**Poor**|1–39%|
|**Missing or no assessable evidence**|0%|

For example, an 80% rating in a 20-point dimension earns **16 points**. The total is rounded to the nearest whole point.

## 2. Assessment Criteria

### Criterion 1 — Essential Coverage and Teaching Story: 20 Points

**What matters:** Whether the presentation covers the essential ideas and connects them into a complete, purposeful lesson.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Level**|**Description**|
|**Outstanding**|Essential ideas across the agreed module are covered at appropriate depth. A purposeful opening, clear progression, meaningful transitions, and strong conclusion form a complete teaching story. Supporting detail is selected carefully without leaving conceptual gaps.|
|**Excellent**|Essential coverage is complete and the teaching sequence is clear. The opening and conclusion serve the lesson well. Only minor transitions or choices of emphasis need improvement.|
|**Good**|Most essential ideas are covered in a generally logical order. Some sections feel like lists of topics, or connections and the overall takeaway need strengthening.|
|**Fair**|Important ideas or connections are missing. The sequence is uneven, with abrupt jumps, unnecessary repetition, or excessive detail that obscures the main story.|
|**Poor**|Major gaps or disconnected fragments prevent the audience from understanding the module as a whole. The lesson’s purpose and conclusion are unclear.|

**Evidence to inspect:** Opening question or problem, learning goals, roadmap, section sequence, transitions, and final synthesis.

A concise deck can earn full marks. Slide count and the amount of text do not establish completeness.

### Criterion 2 — Accuracy and Explanatory Quality: 20 Points

**What matters:** Whether the slides and notes explain the subject correctly and make important ideas understandable.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Level**|**Description**|
|**Outstanding**|Explanations, diagrams, examples, and demonstrations are accurate and accessible. Abstract ideas become understandable through concrete illustrations and carefully developed steps. Likely misconceptions and the limits of simplified models are addressed where relevant.|
|**Excellent**|Content is accurate and well explained, with effective examples and appropriate terminology. Minor clarifications would improve the lesson but do not obstruct understanding.|
|**Good**|Content is mostly correct and understandable. Some jargon, skipped reasoning, weak examples, or unexplained visuals require attention.|
|**Fair**|Recurring inaccuracies or unclear explanations make important ideas difficult to follow. Examples sometimes illustrate the topic without explaining how it works.|
|**Poor**|Fundamental misconceptions, incorrect demonstrations, or unsupported claims undermine the lesson. Definitions are presented without enough explanation to develop understanding.|

**Evidence to inspect:** Concept explanations, worked examples, diagrams, code traces, calculations, demonstration outcomes, and source attribution.

**“Show, not tell” applies here:** a visual should reveal a mechanism, relationship, or result. Decorative imagery does not substitute for explanation.

### Criterion 3 — Visual Design and Classroom Readability: 20 Points

**What matters:** Whether projected slides communicate clearly to a large audience.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Level**|**Description**|
|**Outstanding**|Slides remain readable at classroom viewing distances. Clear hierarchy, purposeful visuals, strong contrast, and disciplined layouts guide attention. Complex information is revealed or divided into manageable steps. Animation, where used, supports understanding.|
|**Excellent**|Text and visuals are consistently readable and well organized. Formatting and layout support the teaching, with only minor density or placement issues.|
|**Good**|Most slides are readable and usable, but some contain dense text, small labels, weak screenshots, or inconsistent layouts.|
|**Fair**|Frequent overcrowding, small text, poor contrast, or unclear diagrams make the projected content difficult to follow. Effects sometimes distract from the explanation.|
|**Poor**|Serious readability and layout problems obstruct teaching. Slides resemble document pages, or important content cannot reasonably be read from the audience’s position.|

**Evidence to inspect:** Slideshow view, text size, diagram labels, code, tables, screenshot cropping, visual hierarchy, proximity, alignment, repetition, and contrast.

The suggested **28–32 pt or larger main text** is a practical starting point, not an automatic scoring rule. Actual readability matters.

Presenter notes may carry supporting detail, but they cannot compensate for unreadable audience-facing slides.

### Criterion 4 — Audience Engagement and Participation: 15 Points

**What matters:** Whether the lesson gives learners reasons to pay attention and opportunities to think.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Level**|**Description**|
|**Outstanding**|A compelling opening and well-placed questions, challenges, or demonstrations sustain curiosity. Participation requires meaningful thinking, suits a large class, and includes clear instructions, realistic thinking time, and useful debriefs. The ending provides a memorable payoff.|
|**Excellent**|The lesson is engaging and includes purposeful participation at suitable points. Activities are practical for a large audience and include clear feedback, with minor improvements possible.|
|**Good**|Some effective engagement and participation are present, but their distribution, depth, timing, or feedback is uneven.|
|**Fair**|Engagement relies mainly on occasional jokes or decorative effects. Questions often require only recall, and activities lack clear instructions, thinking time, or debriefs.|
|**Poor**|The design gives the audience little opportunity to think or participate. Entertainment distracts from learning, or activities are impractical for the classroom setting.|

**Evidence to inspect:** Opening hook, prediction questions, comparisons, short discussions, error-finding tasks, answer reveals, debriefs, and conclusion.

Apply the three presentation rules:

·         

**Cut to the chase:** Reach meaningful content promptly.

·         

·         

**Show, not tell:** Use examples and demonstrations to make ideas visible.

·         

·         

**Save the best for last:** Build toward a strong final application or insight.

·         

Humor, drama, memes, and recurring characters are options—not requirements. Curiosity and well-designed challenges can be equally engaging.

### Criterion 5 — Presenter Notes and Teaching Readiness: 15 Points

**What matters:** Whether another prepared team member could teach the intended lesson using the submitted materials.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Level**|**Description**|
|**Outstanding**|Notes clearly support explanations, transitions, questions, expected answers, and activity timing. Demonstration steps and fallback materials are ready. Files and media work, and the package makes the intended teaching easy to understand and carry out.|
|**Excellent**|Notes provide sufficient teaching guidance, and required files and media are usable. Only minor omissions in timing, transitions, or demonstration support remain.|
|**Good**|Notes explain most important slides, but some teaching steps, answers, or activity instructions must be inferred. Minor technical or packaging issues remain.|
|**Fair**|Notes are sparse, repeat slide text, or omit important explanations and answers. Demonstrations and supporting files require substantial preparation before use.|
|**Poor**|Notes are absent or largely unhelpful. Essential media or files are missing or unusable, leaving the intended lesson dependent on an unsubmitted oral explanation.|

**Evidence to inspect:** Presenter notes, activity instructions, expected answers, approximate timings, demonstration setup, media playback, and offline fallbacks.

A word-for-word script is not required. Brief, useful notes are preferable to long passages that give little practical guidance. Simple title or transition slides need no unnecessary commentary.

### Criterion 6 — Coherence Across the Learning System: 10 Points

**What matters:** Whether the slides reinforce the Study Guide and Video Tutorials while fulfilling their own classroom role.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Level**|**Description**|
|**Outstanding**|The resources form a unified learning experience. Concepts, terminology, examples, and answers agree. Specific references guide learners to practice or further explanation. A shared identity is recognizable, while content is thoughtfully adapted to each medium.|
|**Excellent**|Strong consistency and useful connections exist across the resources. The slides have a clear classroom purpose and share the project’s identity, with only minor mismatches.|
|**Good**|Resources are broadly consistent and share some visual or thematic elements. Connections are limited or generic, and some material is repeated without adapting it to the medium.|
|**Fair**|Terminology, examples, style, or references vary noticeably. The slides feel loosely connected to the guide and videos, or largely reproduce pages from another resource.|
|**Poor**|Significant contradictions or disconnected design make the resources feel like unrelated products. Their roles and connections are unclear.|

**Evidence to inspect:** Consistent definitions and answers, shared examples, section and video references, activity connections, tone, and visual identity.

Coherence means more than using the same colors. Learners should be able to move between resources without losing the thread of the subject.

## 3. Evidence and Fair Assessment

**Assess the submitted teaching resource.** This rubric evaluates the design and readiness of the slides, notes, and supporting files. It does not infer speaking ability, confidence, or classroom delivery that has not been observed.

**Coverage is judged against the module’s essential ideas.** The slides need not reproduce the Study Guide’s detail, but necessary reasoning and connections must remain intact.

**V1.0 must already be usable.** Minor refinements may remain. Planned examples, activities, notes, or demonstrations receive no credit until included.

**Technology earns credit through its teaching value.** Animation, videos, interactive platforms, and sophisticated graphics are useful only when they improve the lesson. A simple, effective visual can earn full marks.

**AI use does not itself reduce the score.** Significant assistance must be documented in the project and personal logs, with evidence beside or below the relevant entry. Missing documentation affects the relevant process or contribution assessment under the Shared Assignment Rules; errors in the submitted slides are assessed under the applicable product criterion.

**Avoid duplicate penalties.** Assess a shortcoming primarily under the criterion it affects. Distinct consequences may be considered separately—for example, an incorrect diagram and text too small to read.
