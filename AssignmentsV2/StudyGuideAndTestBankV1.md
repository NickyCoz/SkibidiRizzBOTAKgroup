**1. General Instructions**

Create a Study Guide and Test Bank that help beginners **explore, understand, practise, and enjoy learning your module**.

**The Study Guide must be fun to use. It is not a textbook intended mainly for passive reading.** Design an experience in which learners solve problems, try tools, make predictions, experiment, and discover why the ideas matter.

Develop the guide from your shared Markdown Content V2.0. Combine clear explanations with concrete activities, useful media, and opportunities to check understanding. The Test Bank should support review and reveal gaps in learning.

Use an engaging, consistent identity across your guide, slides, videos, and supplements. Your language, visuals, recurring characters, and activity design should feel like parts of the same learning experience.

**V1.0 must be a usable, substantially complete first version** covering the core topics in your agreed scope.

Read the **Shared Assignment Rules**, the relevant rubric when provided, and any feedback files supplied with this assignment. The quality checklist is included below.

**A. Organize a Coherent Learning Experience**

Include:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Component**|**What it should provide**|
|**Identification and navigation**|Title, team, module, version, and a usable table of contents.|
|**Learning goals and route**|What learners will learn, assumed prerequisites, and a suggested sequence through explanations, activities, and resources.|
|**Core explanations**|Accurate, beginner-friendly explanations of all core topics.|
|**Examples and activities**|Concrete problems, demonstrations, experiments, or challenges that make the ideas understandable.|
|**Feedback and review**|Hints, explained answers, expected outcomes, summaries, and a glossary.|
|**Resources and references**|Acknowledged sources and selected learning resources with brief introductions and instructions for use.|

Build each major section around a useful question, problem, or challenge. A possible sequence is:

**Encounter a problem → predict or try → observe → explain → apply → check understanding.**

Adapt the sequence to the topic. Learners should always know what they are doing, why it matters, and what they should learn from it.

The guide must contain the essential explanations and activity instructions. A collection of links, copied slides, or disconnected summaries is insufficient.

**B. Use Concrete, Hands-On Activities**

Include meaningful activities throughout the guide. Each major topic should give learners an opportunity to use the idea, rather than only read its definition.

Activities can use paper, cards, role-play, diagrams, existing platforms, or code. Choose methods appropriate to beginners and provide enough guidance to make progress.

For each substantial activity, specify:

· **Goal:** What concept or skill will learners develop?

· **Preparation:** What knowledge, files, tools, or setup do they need?

· **Task:** What should they do, change, predict, or build?

· **Observation:** What should they record or compare?

· **Reflection:** How does the result connect to the concept?

· **Feedback:** What answer, explanation, hint, or expected outcome helps them check their learning?

Examples include:

**Planning and problem-solving through the farmer–wolf–goat–cabbage puzzle**

Let learners attempt the crossing using cards, a diagram, or an interactive version. Then guide them to identify states, legal actions, constraints, and the goal. Ask them to record a valid plan and explain why some tempting moves fail. Use their experience to introduce systematic problem-solving and state-space search.

**Symbolic AI through a small Prolog system**

Introduce a manageable subset of **Prolog using SWI-Prolog**, such as facts, rules, and queries. Guide learners to build a small family-relation, classification, or troubleshooting system. Ask them to predict query results, add a rule, test cases, and explain how conclusions follow from the facts and rules.

This activity should develop both an understanding of rule-based AI and computational thinking: representing information precisely, decomposing a problem, expressing relationships, and checking results.

**Do not turn an introductory activity into an unexplained programming assignment.** Supply starter examples, explain unfamiliar syntax, and require learners to make and explain meaningful changes.

These are examples, not activities that every team must include. Select activities that fit your module.

**C. Build Activities Around Relevant Interactive Platforms**

Where suitable, use established platforms to make invisible processes visible and give learners something to investigate.

The following are starting points, not a mandatory list:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|**Platform or resource**|**Possible activity**|
|[Machine Learning for Kids](https://machinelearningforkids.co.uk/)|Train a small classifier, test unfamiliar examples, investigate mistakes, and improve the training data.|
|[TensorFlow Playground](https://playground.tensorflow.org/)|Predict how changing a network or learning setting will affect its behavior, change one factor at a time, and compare decision boundaries and training/test loss.|
|**ELIZA or a suitable A.L.I.C.E. simulation**|Try carefully chosen inputs, identify recurring response patterns, and investigate where the conversation breaks down. Explain how the particular system works and what its responses do—and do not—demonstrate.|
|[Little Man Computer](https://www.peterhigginson.co.uk/lmc/)|Run a small program step by step, track changes in memory and registers, predict an output, and modify the program. Explain that this is a simplified instructional model.|
|[SWI-Prolog](https://www.swi-prolog.org/) **/** [SWISH](https://swish.swi-prolog.org/)|Build and query a small knowledge base, add rules, and investigate unexpected results.|

Machine Learning for Kids provides hands-on machine-learning projects; TensorFlow Playground exposes network and training controls; Little Man Computer is an instructional CPU simulator; and SWISH provides an online Prolog environment.

An ELIZA exploration can begin with the [ELIZA historical project](https://findingeliza.org/), which includes a “Try ELIZA” section.

**A platform link alone is not an activity.** Provide a specific starting point, instructions, investigation questions, and an explanation of what learners should discover.

Test the exact activity yourselves. Identify the version or simulation used where this affects behavior. Do not assume that every implementation has the same controls or produces identical results.

**D. Curate Videos and Other Learning Resources**

Include well-chosen links to YouTube videos, tutorials, interactive explanations, or other online [learning materials](https://lexue.bit.edu.cn/mod/folder/view.php?id=544488 "Learning materials") where they improve the experience.

Introduce each resource briefly:

· **What is it?** Give its title, creator or provider, and working link.

· **Why use it?** Explain its relevance to the current topic.

· **Which part matters?** Identify a video timestamp range or relevant section where appropriate.

· **What should learners do?** Give a viewing question, prediction, or follow-up task.

· **What should they watch out for?** Note important simplifications or limitations.

A short, humorous video can lighten the mood and make an idea memorable. Place it where it supports the learning sequence.

**Curate rather than accumulate.** A few useful resources with clear introductions are better than a long list of unexplained links. Watch or inspect every recommended resource yourselves.

Consider language, captions, prerequisites, and whether classmates can access the resource on their usual devices and networks. Provide an alternative explanation or activity when access may be difficult.

**E. Make It Enjoyable and Build a Consistent Identity**

Use appropriate humor, storytelling, drama, dialogue, internet memes, surprising examples, recurring characters, or small challenges to sustain interest.

For example, you could organize activities as cases in a detective agency, missions for a confused robot, or problems faced by a fictional computing laboratory.

Choose an approach that suits the topic and audience. You do not need to use every technique.

Maintain consistency in:

· The guide’s name, theme, and visual style.

· Tone of voice and terminology.

· Recurring characters or narrative elements.

· Headings, activity labels, feedback, and navigation.

· Related slides, videos, and supplements.

**This consistent identity is the beginning of your learning resource’s brand.** It should help learners recognize the materials and know what kind of experience to expect.

Humor and entertainment should support understanding. Avoid jokes that require obscure cultural knowledge without explanation, distracting decoration, or stories so long that learners lose the main idea. Acknowledge borrowed media and distinguish playful analogies from technical explanations.

Use readable layouts and purposeful visuals. Place related explanations, figures, instructions, and feedback close together.

**F. Create a Balanced Test Bank**

Submit a separate Test Bank covering the core concepts taught in the guide.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Question type**|**Minimum**|**Expectations**|
|**True/False**|20|Test meaningful understanding with clear, unambiguous statements.|
|**Multiple Choice**|20|Use plausible alternatives and specify whether one or multiple answers should be selected.|
|**Open-ended**|6|Require explanation, application, comparison, analysis, or evaluation supported by reasoning or examples.|

Open-ended questions should invite developed responses—normally more than 100 words, or equivalent reasoning through diagrams, calculations, or worked steps.

Connect some questions to the guide’s activities: interpret an experiment, explain a failed plan, predict a program’s behavior, or justify a design decision. Include enough information for the question to remain answerable without opening an external platform.

Number questions consistently and identify their topic or corresponding guide section. Balance coverage across important ideas, avoiding repetition and excessive attention to minor details.

Place questions first and a clearly labelled answer key later. Include:

· Correct answers and brief explanations for True/False questions, correcting false statements.

· Correct choices and reasoning for multiple-choice questions.

· Sample answers or expected key points for open-ended questions, recognizing valid alternatives.

**G. Verify Content and Learning Activities**

Have another member attempt and review every test-bank question. Test the examples, activity instructions, code, and platform settings yourselves.

Ask someone who did not write a selected section to follow it as a learner. Check whether they understand the instructions, can complete the task, and can explain the intended idea afterward.

Correct inaccurate claims, confusing steps, misleading feedback, and broken resources. Keep terminology and answers consistent with Markdown Content V2.0, slides, and videos.

Record significant checks and revisions in the project and personal logs, with evidence **beside or below the relevant entry**.
