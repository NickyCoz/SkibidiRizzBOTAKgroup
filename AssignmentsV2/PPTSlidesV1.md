**A. Tell a Complete Teaching Story**

Cover the essential ideas across your agreed module scope. Detailed extensions may remain in the Study Guide, but the slides must preserve the connections needed to understand the whole topic.

Include:

· **An engaging opening:** A question, problem, scenario, or demonstration that gives the audience a reason to care.

· **Learning goals and a brief roadmap:** What will the audience understand or be able to do?

· **A logical sequence of core ideas:** Introduce prerequisites before using them and make transitions clear.

· **Concrete examples and demonstrations:** Show how important ideas work.

· **Audience participation:** Give learners opportunities to predict, decide, explain, or solve.

· **A meaningful conclusion:** Resolve the opening question where appropriate, connect the main ideas, and provide a clear takeaway.

· **Next steps:** Point learners to relevant practice, Study Guide sections, or Video Tutorials.

**A complete story is more than a list of definitions.** The audience should understand how each part contributes to the overall explanation.

For example, a symbolic-AI presentation might begin with a puzzle, introduce states and rules, demonstrate a search for a solution, and conclude by explaining both the strengths and limitations of that approach.

There is no fixed slide count. Use enough slides to explain the material clearly without padding or overcrowding. If the module needs several teaching sessions, divide it into clearly named parts with a coherent overall sequence.

This assignment covers the module’s teaching slides. It is broader than the separate mini lecture, which focuses on one selected question.

**B. Design for a Large Classroom**

Your slides must be understandable when projected to a large audience, including students sitting at the back.

· Give each slide one main teaching purpose.

· Use short phrases and concise statements where possible.

· Keep paragraphs, full scripts, and detailed explanations in presenter notes.

· Use large, readable text. As a practical starting point, aim for approximately **28–32 pt or larger for main text**, then check the projected result.

· Maintain strong contrast between text and background.

· Use consistent headings, fonts, colors, spacing, and alignment.

· Make diagrams, labels, tables, and code large enough to inspect.

· Crop screenshots to the relevant area and highlight what the audience should notice.

· Divide complex visuals or processes into manageable steps.

· Use animation only when its timing helps explain a relationship, process, or change.

**Do not solve a crowded slide by shrinking everything.** Simplify, split the material, or move supporting detail to the notes or Study Guide.

Apply **proximity, alignment, repetition, and contrast**. Place labels close to the parts they explain, and make the most important information visually prominent.

Test the slides in full-screen slideshow mode. Where possible, check them on the classroom projector from the back of the room.

**C. Make Important Ideas Visible**

Choose visuals that explain the subject, such as:

· A diagram showing relationships or system components.

· A sequence showing how an algorithm or process unfolds.

· A comparison highlighting meaningful differences.

· An annotated example or short code trace.

· A demonstration showing how changing an input affects the result.

Explain what the audience should notice. Decorative images alone do not make an abstract idea easier to understand.

For live demonstrations or interactive platforms:

· Establish the question before starting.

· Prepare the starting state and necessary files.

· Show only the steps relevant to the learning goal.

· Explain the result afterward.

· Include screenshots or a short recording as a fallback.

A demonstration should help the audience follow the concept, rather than spend time watching the presenter navigate menus or solve setup problems.

**D. Involve the Audience**

Include brief learning checks and activities at meaningful points in the presentation.

Suitable formats for a large class include:

· Predict the next step or output.

· Choose between alternatives by raising hands.

· Discuss a question briefly with a neighbor.

· Identify an error in an example.

· Trace a few steps of an algorithm.

· Compare two possible solutions and justify a choice.

Give clear instructions and allow realistic thinking time. Provide the answer, explanation, or debrief after the audience has attempted the task.

Place answers on a later slide, reveal them at the appropriate time, or provide them in presenter notes. Avoid showing the answer before learners have an opportunity to think.

Activities should work for a large audience without requiring lengthy individual responses. If an activity needs devices, internet access, or a particular platform, state this in the notes and provide a workable alternative.

**E. Use the Three Rules of Presentation**

**1. Cut to the chase.**

Begin with something meaningful. Keep introductions and roadmaps brief, and move promptly to the question or problem that drives the lesson.

**2. Show, not tell.**

Demonstrate the mechanism, example, or result. Instead of merely saying “binary search is efficient,” show how the search space changes.

**3. Save the best for last.**

Build toward a strong final application, demonstration, or insight that brings the story together. Teach necessary foundations early so the audience can appreciate the ending.

Design the presentation to sustain curiosity. Humor, drama, recurring characters, memes, and surprising examples may help, provided they support understanding and suit the audience.

Maintain the same recognizable identity as the Study Guide and Video Tutorials. Consistent colors and fonts help, but so do a shared tone, recurring examples, and familiar ways of introducing activities and giving feedback.

**F. Provide Useful Presenter Notes**

Include presenter notes for slides that need explanation beyond what appears on screen.

Notes should give another team member enough guidance to teach the material. Include, where relevant:

· The explanation and key points to emphasize.

· Transitions between ideas.

· Steps for demonstrating an example.

· Questions to ask and expected answers.

· Instructions and approximate timing for activities.

· Common misconceptions or likely audience questions.

· Sources, media credits, and related resource locations.

**The slides and notes together must make the intended teaching understandable.** Do not assume that the teacher will hear an unsubmitted oral explanation.

Notes may use clear bullet points; a word-for-word script is not required. Simple title or transition slides do not need unnecessary commentary.

**G. Connect the Slides to the Learning System**

Develop the slides from the checked Markdown content, and keep them consistent with the Study Guide, Test Bank, and Video Tutorials.

Use specific references where they help learners continue:

· “Study Guide §3.2 — Try the planning challenge.”

· “Video 02 — Watch the rule-based system demonstration.”

· “Test Bank Q12–Q15 — Check your understanding.”

Use the actual section titles, filenames, or question numbers in your materials.

Reuse helpful examples or characters across resources, adapting their treatment to the medium. For example:

· The slides introduce a maze and compare two strategies visually.

· The Study Guide lets learners trace or modify the strategies.

· A video demonstrates the process and explains a common mistake.

· The Test Bank asks learners to predict behavior in a new maze.

**Each resource must make its own contribution while remaining part of the same learning system.**

When an explanation or answer changes, correct every affected resource. Keep learner-facing references accurate as the materials develop.
