**1. General Instructions**

Create one or more prerecorded tutorials that help beginners understand your module through **clear explanations, demonstrations, and engaging audiovisual storytelling**.

You may use your PPT Slides as the basis for narrated presentation videos. You may also create dedicated videos for particularly important or difficult ideas, using screen recordings, demonstrations, animations, physical objects, dialogue, drama, or other suitable formats.

**Do not simply read the slides aloud.** Your narration should explain, illustrate, question, and connect ideas. The visuals should show what viewers need to understand.

Aim for the experience of a good educational YouTube video: viewers understand why the topic matters, want to see what happens next, and feel that their time is well spent.

**Quality matters more than quantity—both the number of videos and their total duration.** Hours spent recording do not automatically produce useful [learning materials](https://lexue.bit.edu.cn/mod/folder/view.php?id=544488 "Learning materials"). A concise, carefully designed tutorial is more valuable than a long recording that viewers struggle to finish.

Videos offer opportunities to express creativity and arouse curiosity, surprise, amusement, empathy, and a sense of achievement. Use these opportunities to make learning memorable.

Together, your **Study Guide and Test Bank, PPT Slides, and Video Tutorials** should form a complete, beginner-friendly learning system. Each resource should reinforce the others while making good use of its medium.

Read the **Shared Assignment Rules** and relevant feedback files. The quality checklist is included below.

**2. Specific Requirements**

**Assignment type:** Team assignment — one submission per team

**Number of videos:** **1–8 videos in total**  
**Maximum size:** **250 MB per video file, with no exceptions**

**A. Plan a Focused Tutorial Collection**

Organize the videos into a clear learning sequence through your module’s essential ideas.

You may choose:

· One complete presentation-based tutorial.

· A series of presentation-based tutorials.

· A combination of presentation videos and dedicated concept or demonstration videos.

· Another coherent format that serves the learning goals.

**Eight videos is the maximum, not a target.** You do not need to produce six or eight videos if fewer can teach the material effectively.

Avoid splitting the content into many trivial videos, such as separate recordings for individual definitions or closely related points. Combine related ideas into meaningful lessons.

The collection need not reproduce every detail in the Study Guide. It should provide a connected explanation of the essential ideas, with prerequisites introduced or clearly identified.

Consider dedicated videos where a concept benefits from closer explanation—for example, tracing an algorithm, building a small rule-based system, or comparing a robot’s behavior under different strategies.

Give each video a clear purpose and descriptive title. Number multiple videos in the intended viewing order.

**Plan the number, scope, duration, and file size together before recording.** Leave room for demonstrations and explanations without producing oversized files or an unnecessarily fragmented collection.

V1.0 must contain actual, usable tutorials. Scripts, storyboards, trailers, and plans for unrecorded videos do not replace finished videos.

**B. Explain Beyond the Slides**

When recording with your PPT:

· Explain the reasoning behind the points on screen.

· Use examples, analogies, questions, and demonstrations.

· Direct attention to the relevant part of a diagram or process.

· Reveal information progressively when this helps viewers follow.

· Adapt slides for video viewing where necessary.

**Reading slide text as the main narration does not meet the purpose of this assignment.** Briefly stating a definition or reading a question is acceptable when it supports the explanation.

For example, instead of reading “A stack follows last-in, first-out,” show objects being added and removed, ask which one will come out next, and connect the result to an application such as undo.

Viewers should gain understanding from the explanation, not merely hear the words they can already see.

**C. Give Each Video a Purposeful Structure**

Include:

1. **An opening hook:** A meaningful question, surprising result, problem, or short scenario.

2. **A clear learning promise:** What will viewers understand or be able to do?

3. **A guided explanation:** Develop ideas logically through concrete examples.

4. **A demonstration or application:** Show the idea working.

5. **A learning check:** Invite viewers to predict, solve, explain, or try something.

6. **A satisfying conclusion:** Resolve the main question and reinforce the takeaway.

These elements may be combined naturally; they do not require separate title screens.

Apply the three presentation rules:

· **Cut to the chase:** Reach meaningful content promptly. Keep introductions and branding sequences brief.

· **Show, not tell:** Demonstrate processes, decisions, and results.

· **Save the best for last:** Build toward an application, revealing result, or insight that brings the explanation together.

Give viewers a reason to stay without withholding necessary explanations or making promises the video does not fulfill.

**D. Control Duration, Pacing, and Information Density**

There is no minimum runtime. **Longer videos and more recording time do not earn higher marks.**

Around **5–10 minutes** can be a useful starting point for a focused concept tutorial. Longer videos are acceptable when their content, structure, pacing, and file size justify the length.

For longer videos, provide chapter timestamps so learners can find and revisit specific sections.

During editing:

· Remove repeated explanations, false starts, and verbal filler.

· Cut unnecessary setup, menu navigation, and waiting.

· Keep examples that contribute to understanding.

· Allow time to inspect important visuals and follow difficult reasoning.

· Shorten introductions, credits, and transitions that interrupt the learning.

**Do not make an overlong video “shorter” by speeding up the narration until beginners cannot follow it.** Improve the selection and explanation of the content.

Ask yourselves: _If we were the intended learners, would we want to keep watching? What would we understand or be able to do afterward?_

**E. Use Video and Multimedia Design Principles**

Coordinate what viewers **see, hear, and think about**.

· **Match narration to visuals:** Show the relevant step or object while explaining it.

· **Guide attention:** Use highlighting, labels, arrows, zooming, or a deliberate pointer.

· **Break down complexity:** Introduce processes and diagrams in manageable stages.

· **Remove distractions:** Avoid unrelated footage, decorative effects, and competing sounds.

· **Use readable visuals:** Crop screen recordings and enlarge code, labels, and other important details.

· **Explain transitions:** Make clear why the next part follows.

· **Edit transparently:** If a long process is shortened, explain what has been skipped.

Avoid displaying long paragraphs while narrating the same paragraphs. Concise labels and purposeful visuals usually provide better support. Captions serve a separate accessibility function and may repeat the narration.

**F. Use Creativity and Emotion to Support Learning**

Make the videos enjoyable and worth watching.

Possible approaches include:

· A short drama introducing a problem.

· A recurring character making a common mistake.

· A humorous analogy followed by a precise explanation.

· A mystery resolved through a concept.

· A prediction followed by a surprising demonstration.

· A progression from an unsuccessful attempt to a working solution.

Choose approaches that help learners understand, remember, or care about the idea.

Maintain a recognizable identity across the videos, slides, and Study Guide: consistent tone, terminology, visuals, recurring examples, and character roles where used.

Humor and memes should be understandable to the intended audience. Explain the limits of analogies and avoid entertainment that overwhelms the lesson.

**Production complexity is not the goal.** A clear screen recording with thoughtful narration can be highly effective. Appearing on camera is optional.

**G. Make Viewers Active Learners**

Include opportunities to pause and think or try something independently.

For example:

· “Pause and predict the next output.”

· “Which move would make this plan fail?”

· “Change this rule and test your prediction.”

· “Try the example before watching the solution.”

Provide enough information to attempt the task, then explain the answer or expected result.

For demonstrations, show both **what to do and why it works**. Identify important settings, starting conditions, inputs, and outputs.

Connect activities to specific Study Guide sections, Test Bank questions, or supporting files. Avoid vague directions such as “see our other materials.”

**H. Ensure Clear Audio and Visuals**

Check that:

· Speech is intelligible and volume remains reasonably consistent.

· Background noise is controlled.

· Narration is conversational and appropriately expressive.

· Music, if used, does not compete with speech.

· Text, code, diagrams, and demonstrated actions are readable.

· Technical terms and names are pronounced correctly.

· Captions, where included, are accurate and do not obscure important content.

Captions are strongly encouraged and should be included where needed for understanding and accessibility. Correct automatically generated captions, particularly technical terminology.

If synthetic narration or other AI-generated media is used, check pronunciation, pacing, accuracy, and coordination with the visuals. Document significant AI assistance in the logs.

Professional equipment is not required. Clear communication and careful checking matter more.

**I. Compress Videos Properly**

**Every submitted video must be an MP4 file no larger than 250 MB. There are no exceptions.**

Treat compression as part of production. The aim is a file that is **small enough to submit and convenient to use while preserving the quality needed for learning**.

· Export with suitable video-compression settings.

· Choose resolution, frame rate, and bitrate appropriate to the content.

· Avoid unnecessarily large exports when they offer no visible learning benefit.

· Preserve readable text, clear diagrams, visible actions, and intelligible speech.

· Test a short representative export before recording or exporting the entire collection.

· Check the actual file size after export and leave a margin below the limit.

**A ZIP file is not a substitute for proper video compression.** Do not rely on compressing an oversized MP4 into an archive.

If a video is too large, revise the content, improve the export settings, or reorganize the lesson within the **eight-video maximum**. Do not split it into arbitrary fragments simply to bypass the limit.

Watch the final compressed version—not only the original recording. Check detailed screenshots, small labels, moving demonstrations, captions, and audio. Compression that makes the lesson difficult to follow is unacceptable.

**J. Integrate, Review, and Verify**

Keep definitions, examples, and answers consistent with Markdown Content V2.0, the Study Guide, and the slides.

For example:

· The slides introduce a problem visually.

· The video demonstrates the reasoning or process.

· The Study Guide provides a related activity.

· The Test Bank checks whether learners can apply the idea in a new situation.

Have another team member watch **every final compressed video from beginning to end**. Ask someone who did not create a selected tutorial to follow it as a beginner and attempt its learning check.

Correct factual errors, confusing steps, unnecessary length, unreadable visuals, audio problems, and inaccurate captions. Carry content corrections into the other affected resources.

Record significant testing, feedback, revisions, and AI use in the project and personal logs, with evidence **beside or below the relevant entry**.
